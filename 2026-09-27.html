一句话介绍： Opus写了一个会自己表演的网页 然后一帧一帧给它拍照，再把照片连起来。

整个过程分五步：

✅第一步：把真软件请进网页 Omia 的界面是网页技术写的。 我让 AI 直接把源码里的侧栏、编辑器、各种文件查看器拿出来，装进一个网页里跑。 读文件的后台换成一个「替身」，Omia 自己察觉不到。 所以片子里打开的 PDF、CAD 图纸、SketchUp 模型，都是 Omia 真的在打开。

✅第二步：给每一秒写剧本 写一张时间表：第几秒打开哪个文件、光标移到哪、点哪个菜单、字幕出哪句。 网页只认一条规矩：告诉它「现在是第几秒」，它就摆成那一秒的样子。

✅第三步：让镜头动起来 每扇 Omia 窗口都能在 3D 空间里摆放、倾斜、推近。 长廊那段，是 16 个真 Omia 窗口排成走廊，整条走廊往镜头这边移，看起来就像在往里飞。

✅第四步：一帧一帧拍照 浏览器从第 0 秒开始，每次往前走 1/30 秒，拍一张。 91 秒一共 2730 张，电脑自己拍了大约 13 分钟。

✅第五步：声音也是算出来的 英文旁白是我本地的声音模型读的，读完再让语音识别听一遍，确认每句都对。 配乐没有用任何现成的曲子，是 Python 用公式算出来的鼓、贝斯和钢琴。 音乐和画面按同一张时间表写，镜头一切，鼓点就在那儿。

这也是很强了～～

30 mistakes I see enterprises make with AI transformation: 1. Buying AI…

Alex Prompter @alex_prompter

30 mistakes I see enterprises make with AI transformation:

1. Buying AI licences and calling it a strategy. Decide which problems you want to solve, how people will use the tools and what improvement you expect to see. Access alone doesn’t answer those questions.

2. Expecting every employee to become an AI engineer. People need different levels of training and responsibility. Helping someone use AI in their work doesn’t automatically prepare them to build and maintain a system for others.

3. Asking AI for answers before agreeing on what a good answer looks like. Start with real examples and clear criteria. Keep verified corrections as test cases, and rerun those tests when you change the system.

4. Blaming the model before checking the whole setup. A failure can come from the model, instructions, missing information, tools or the surrounding software. Investigate where it went wrong before deciding what to replace.

5. Automating a process nobody can explain from start to finish. We spoke to a team whose work moved between calls, emails, spreadsheets and shared folders. Understanding how the work actually got done was a substantial job in itself.

6. Giving an agent more access than its job requires. Limit what it can read and change, and require approval for consequential actions. Enforce those permissions in the software. An instruction telling the agent to be careful is not an access control.

Researchers at UT Arlington just published a paper showing that AI agents miss requirements even when they mark the task as complete.

They call it the understanding–execution gap: a requirement can be available to the agent without being carried through into the result.

They extracted 509 requirements from the instructions agents could see. Across seven models, around 80–86% were satisfied. Those are individual requirements, so meeting most of them doesn’t necessarily mean completing the task.

Imagine asking an agent to prepare overdue payment reminders. Three instructions: use the latest payment records, exclude disputed invoices and wait for approval before sending.

The agent lists all three in its plan. Then it produces a tidy spreadsheet and well-written emails. The amounts add up. Everything looks ready.

Except two customers paid this morning, and one invoice is under dispute.

The agent’s plan was reasonable. Most of the work was useful. But if those emails go out, the missed requirements become customer problems.

Checking that the spreadsheet exists or that the emails read well won’t catch this. You need to check each account against the current payment records, verify that disputed invoices are excluded and keep sending blocked until approval.

Most things people call an AI agent are actually one of six coordination…

Alex Prompter @alex_prompter

Most things people call an AI agent are actually one of six coordination patterns, and most people only know one of them. The model underneath is almost secondary. The pattern is what actually decides what the system can do.

Prompt chaining runs a fixed sequence, input, step one, a check, step two, output. A failed check stops the chain outright instead of letting it continue on bad output. This is the right call when each step depends completely on the last one being correct.

Routing looks at the input first, then sends it down the right path, a cheap model, a stronger model, or a tool call, before merging into one output. Classify first, then specialize, instead of running every input through the same expensive path.

Parallelization sends the same input to multiple calls at once, then merges or votes on the results into a single answer. Useful when you want several independent attempts instead of trusting one pass to get it right.

Orchestrator assigns work to several workers in the moment rather than planning every step in advance, then combines their results. This fits tasks where you know the goal but not the exact breakdown until the work is already underway.

Evaluator loop has one step generate an answer and another grade it. A rejected answer loops back with notes until it actually passes, instead of shipping the first attempt regardless of quality.

Autonomous is the loosest pattern, an agent acts, gets feedback from its environment, and repeats with no fixed script, deciding on its own when the task is actually finished.

Knowing which of these six you are actually building changes what you can promise it will do. Calling all of it an agent and stopping there is how teams end up debugging a system they never actually designed.
