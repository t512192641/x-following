从「用 AI 工具」到 agentic 组织的七个阶段：工具采用 → 上下文层 → Harness 与评测 → 自动化上生产 → 规模化治理 → 围绕业务流程串 agent → 衡量整条流程

Alex Prompter @alex_prompter

Your company can adopt AI everywhere and still take just as long to get anything done.

People produce work faster, then wait for someone to review it, move it into another system or start the next step. The individual tasks improve while the process barely changes.

When I think about the move from AI tools to an agentic organisation, I break it into seven stages:

1. Tool adoption

People use AI to help with individual tasks. They still provide the context, start the work and decide what happens with the result.

This is a useful place to begin. It also reveals which recurring tasks might be worth building into a reliable workflow.

2. The context layer

Give agents access to the information they need: business rules, customer records, product knowledge and previous decisions.

That might come through documents, databases, search or APIs. Keep ownership and permissions clear so agents can retrieve relevant information without gaining access to everything.

3. Harness engineering and evals

Define what successful completion looks like. Start with real tasks, checked outcomes and the exceptions your team regularly handles.

Build the routing, tools and controls around those requirements, then evaluate the model and harness together.

Repeat trials to check consistency. Rerun relevant evals when you change the system, and turn production failures into new test cases. This gives you evidence for decisions about quality, model choice and cost.

4. Put automations into production

Move useful automations into managed services that can run on a schedule or respond to an event without depending on someone’s laptop or personal login.

Give them monitoring, failure handling and an owner who responds when something breaks.

An automation that works for its creator still needs engineering before other people can depend on it.

5. Manage governance at scale

Permissions and oversight should already be in place. As usage grows, maintain an inventory of agents, their owners, what they can access and whether they still serve a purpose.

Review performance and permissions regularly. Investigate deterioration, and retire agents that no longer add value, including removing their access.

6. Connect agents around a business process

Where multiple agents are useful, define what each handoff must contain and what happens when the next step cannot proceed.

Use code to check requirements you can verify deterministically. Use model judges for criteria that need interpretation, calibrated against human review.

Test the handoffs as well as the individual agents.

7. Run and measure the complete workflow

Coordinate the process across agents, conventional software and people, including retries, approvals and exceptions.

Evaluate whether the whole task gets completed correctly. Track failures, human intervention and cost per successful completion alongside production monitoring.

We walked a VP of Engineering through this framework. His organisation had more than a hundred tools and was still largely at tool adoption.

My advice in that situation is to choose one valuable workflow and make it dependable from start to finish.

That gives the business something concrete to build on: a process with an owner, measurable results and evidence that it works.
